This guide shows you how to make a shiny car look like it has worked outdoors for years, with a brush and water-based paint. Most mistakes wipe off while wet or paint over once dry.

## First project: weather a ready-to-run car

Get a cheap **ready-to-run** car, one that comes fully built and painted. A secondhand one is fine. Work in a room with good fresh air. You keep the factory paint and add dirt and rust on top. A thin wash adds color without hiding detail, and it wipes off while wet.

**[Weathering](/learn/glossary#weathering)** means adding dirt, rust, and faded paint so a model looks used. Look at photos of real cars first, such as our [trains](/trains). Dirt collects low, and rust starts where water sits.

### Step 1: Wash the car

Wash the car gently in warm, soapy water and let it dry. This removes finger oils that stop paint from sticking.

### Step 2: Brush on a thin wash

A **wash** is very runny paint that flows into seams and corners and adds shadow.

1. On an old plate, mix one drop of dark brown or black paint with about ten drops of water, plus a tiny drop of dish soap so it spreads on shiny factory paint.
2. Brush it onto one side of the car and let it run into seams, around rivets (small raised bumps), and along the bottom.
3. After a minute, wipe the raised areas with a tissue. The color stays in the low spots.
4. Let it dry. Too light? Add another coat.

Repeat on each side, with more grime low and less up high.

### Step 3: Drybrush the edges

**Drybrushing** puts a little color on raised edges.

1. Dip an old, stiff brush in a light color, such as tan for dust or orange-brown for rust.
2. Wipe almost all the paint off on a paper towel, until the brush leaves only a faint mark.
3. Flick the brush lightly across ladders, door edges, and bolt heads.

### Step 4: Seal it (optional)

A brush-on, water-based **flat** (not shiny) clear coat protects your work. Test it on the bottom of the car first.

Keep this car to test each new technique.

## Second project (optional): paint a small part

To paint bare plastic, start small: an unassembled plastic structure kit, or a detail part like a barrel or crate. Small pieces are easier to paint evenly than a whole car.

1. **Clean it.** Wash the parts in soapy water. This removes **mold release**, a slippery factory coating that paint does not stick to.
2. **Prime it.** **Primer** is a first coat that helps paint grip. Use a brush-on, water-based primer labeled for plastic, in light gray. Brush on two light coats.
3. **Paint it.** Add water, one drop at a time, until the paint flows like milk. Brush on two or three thin coats, letting each dry. Thick paint fills in small details and leaves brush marks.
4. **Weather it.** Use the same wash and drybrushing from the first project.
5. **Assemble it.** Glue does not hold well on paint, so scrape paint off the spots where parts join.

## Brushes

- **A fine round brush** for small details
- **A medium round brush** for general painting
- **A small flat brush** for broad areas
- **An old, stiff brush** for drybrushing

Pick synthetic bristles for acrylic paint, and dip only the bristles, not the metal band. When you finish, wash the brush with soap, shape the tip, and let it dry lying flat.

## Decals

A **decal** is printed lettering or a logo. Most are **water-slide decals**, a thin film that slides off its backing paper after a soak in water.

1. Brush a **gloss** (shiny) clear coat where the decal goes, so no air gets trapped under the film.
2. Soak the decal briefly, until it slides on its backing.
3. Brush on a little decal **setting solution**, which helps the decal settle, and slide the decal into place with a soft brush.
4. Let it dry overnight, then brush on a flat clear coat to hide the edges.

## Fixing mistakes

These fixes are for paint you applied yourself. Ask the owner before you work on someone else's model.

- **Wet paint in the wrong spot:** lift it with a damp cotton swab.
- **Too much wash or weathering:** wipe with a damp swab while wet. Once dry, drybrush a little of the car's base color back over it.
- **A run, drip, or speck of dust:** let it dry, sand it smooth with a fine sanding stick, and repaint.
- **A whole part gone wrong:** prime it again and repaint.

Paint stripper is a last resort and can damage plastic; see [Workshop Safety](/learn/workshop-safety#spraying-and-solvents) first.

## What to buy

Buy nothing before your first club visit; the club has shared tools. For your own bench, a small starter set costs roughly $30 to $50. Prices are rough and change over time.

- **A cheap ready-to-run car**, often $10 to $25 secondhand
- **Three synthetic brushes**, about $10 to $20
- **Water-based hobby acrylics**, such as Vallejo, in black, dark brown, tan, and rust, a few dollars a bottle
- **Cotton swabs, paper towels, and an old plate**

Optional upgrades: primer, gloss and flat clear coats, decal setting solution, and weathering powder.

## Paint safely

Brush painting with water-based acrylics needs a room with good fresh air. Keep paint away from food, children, and pets, and do not pour paint down a drain.

Spray cans, airbrushes, solvent paints like enamel and lacquer, and strippers need more protection. See [Workshop Safety](/learn/workshop-safety#spraying-and-solvents) before you use them.
