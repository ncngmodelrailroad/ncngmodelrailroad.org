Everyone makes mistakes, and most can be fixed, repainted, or rebuilt. A few cannot. This guide sorts the work into three groups: try it, ask first, and leave it alone without approval.

## The short version

- Practice on scrap and on your own projects first. Mistakes there are almost always fixable.
- On a shared layout, ask before you touch track, scenery, locomotives, or anyone else's model. A layout is a large model railroad that many people share.
- Leave wiring, glued ballast, factory paint, and decoder settings alone unless you have approval.
- **Volunteering?** You do not need your own tools. [Email us](/contact) before your first session. Details are at the end of this page.

## Try it, ask first, or leave it alone

Questions are welcome. Most experienced members would rather answer one than repair a mistake. A layout has history you cannot see, such as old repairs, hidden wires, or parts that look loose but are meant to move.

**Try it.** A mistake here costs time and materials, not the model.

- Scenery on a practice board, a loose piece of wood or foam that is not part of the layout. You can layer ground cover, rocks, trees, and grass, scrape them off, and start again.
- Your own paint on scrap or on your own projects. Water-based acrylic paint cleans up with water while wet, and you can usually paint over it once dry.
- Loose [ballast](/learn/glossary#ballast) on a practice board. Ballast is the fine gravel under model track. Unglued, it brushes up and moves easily.
- Simple kits. Fit the parts together without glue first, because some glues grab almost at once.
- Dry [weathering](/learn/glossary#weathering) powders on a practice surface. Weathering makes a model look used and dirty. Start light and add more. On a finished model, ask first, because flat (matte) paint can trap the powder.

Practice on cheap things: scrap plastic sheet (styrene), plastic spoons, a used freight car from a swap meet, or clean loose wire for soldering.

**Ask first.** These are not off limits, but a quick question protects the layout.

- Scenery already on the layout, especially near track, wiring, buildings, or [turnouts](/learn/glossary#turnout-switch). A turnout is a track switch that sends a train one way or another. Loose ballast grains can jam turnouts and other moving parts.
- Locomotives, brass models, and small detail parts. A locomotive, the engine that pulls the train, holds a motor and gears. Many also hold a decoder, a small circuit board that lets a [DCC](/learn/glossary#dcc-digital-command-control) (digital) system control it. Brass models are often handmade in small numbers, hard to replace, and their details bend easily.
- Track alignment. A tiny bump where two rails meet can derail train after train. Report the spot instead of adjusting it.
- Painting or weathering a model you did not build.

**Do not change without approval.** Mistakes here can be permanent.

- Factory paint and lettering on layout or member models. Damage can be hard or impossible to match, and thinners or stripping can ruin it.
- Glued ballast and scenery. Once glue sets, stray ballast can lock a turnout or block the rails, and removing it can damage the track.
- Layout wiring and power. A change in one spot can stop trains far away. Do not unplug, cut, or add any wire.
- Decoder settings. Changing or resetting a decoder can erase a locomotive's number (its address), speed, and sound settings if nobody wrote them down. Restoring its software (firmware) or sounds can need the maker's equipment and original files.
- Fragile parts on older kits, which may have no replacement.

Also, never lean on the [benchwork](/learn/glossary#benchwork), the tables and frames that hold up the layout. Do not sit on it or set heavy tools on it.

## Re-railing a car

Cars come off the track (derail) often. It is normal and easy to fix. First, stop the train, or ask the operator to stop it. Ask before you touch a locomotive, a brass model, or anything you do not recognize. For an ordinary car you are allowed to handle:

1. Lift the car by holding it from underneath. Do not squeeze the body or touch the handrails, ladders, or brake wheels.
2. Set one [truck](/learn/glossary#truck) on the rails. A truck is the swiveling frame that holds a set of wheels. Each wheel has a raised inner lip (the flange), and it goes inside the rail.
3. Line up the second truck the same way.
4. Roll the car gently back and forth about an inch. It should roll freely and stay on the rails.
5. If the same car keeps coming off at the same spot, report it. That usually means a track or wheel problem, not something you did.

## Safety basics

- **Blades.** Cut away from your body and your other hand. Use a fresh blade. Cap the knife or set it in a holder when you stop. Put used blades in a hard, closed container.
- **Eyes.** Wear safety glasses when you cut wire, snip metal, drill, use power tools, or solder.
- **Hot tools.** A soldering iron stays hot long after you unplug it. Always put it back in its stand. If it falls, let it fall. Never try to catch it.
- **Fumes.** For glues, paints, and solvents, follow the product label and its safety data sheet (SDS). Brush-painting with water-based acrylics needs only a room with good fresh air. Spraying and solvent products need ventilation that carries fumes outdoors. If you feel a headache, dizziness, or irritation, move to fresh air.
- **Unknown old materials.** Some old solder, cast metal parts, and older paints contain lead. Do not sand, file, drill, or heat an unknown old material until you know what it is.
- **Food.** Keep food and drinks away from the bench. Wipe up dust instead of sweeping it. Wash your hands before you eat or drink.
- **Lifting and limits.** Hold loads close, and do not twist while lifting. Ask for help with anything heavy or awkward. If climbing a ladder or crawling under the benchwork does not suit you, say so.

For more on tools, paint, and soldering, see the [workbench tools](/learn/first-workbench-tools), [painting and weathering](/learn/painting-weathering), and [electronics and DCC](/learn/electronics-dcc-basics) guides.

## Before your first session

You do not need to buy tools before you volunteer. The layout has shared tools, including an NCE Power Cab, a simple DCC controller, at the workbench.

Members run trains with the Engine Driver app on Android tablets, used as handheld throttles (train controls). The tablets connect to JMRI, software on a computer that runs the layout over USB. Running trains can be an easy way to start, so ask whether it is an option for you.

To get ready:

1. [Email us](/contact) before you come to confirm the date and ask what to bring. The [volunteer page](/volunteer) has more.
2. Wear clothes you do not mind getting paint or glue on, and closed-toe shoes. Tie back long hair.
3. Bring reading glasses or a magnifier if you use them, and safety glasses if you have a pair. Bring a phone or notebook for notes and "before" photos.
4. When you arrive, ask: What should I avoid touching? Which shared tools can I use? How do I stop a train? Is there a scrap piece I can practice on?
