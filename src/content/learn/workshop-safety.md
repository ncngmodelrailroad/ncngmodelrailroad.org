Most model railroad work is low risk, and most mistakes are small and fixable. A few tools and products deserve respect, and this page collects them in one place. Read the section for whatever you are about to try.

Where this page and a product label or its **safety data sheet (SDS)** differ, follow the label.

## Every project

- Wear safety glasses marked Z87 when you cut, snip wire, drill, carve, sand, spray, or solder. Snipped bits fly farther than you expect.
- Keep food and drinks off the bench, and wash your hands before you eat.
- Vacuum or wipe up dust instead of sweeping it into the air.
- Cap glues, paints, solvents, and blades. Keep them away from children, pets, and the kitchen.
- Hold loads close and do not twist while lifting. Ask for help with anything heavy, and say so if a ladder or crawling under benchwork does not suit you.

## Glue and blades

- Cut away from your body, with your other hand behind the blade. Replace a dull blade, because it needs more force and slips more.
- Cap the knife or set it in a holder when you put it down.
- Never catch a falling knife. Step back and let it drop.
- Put old blades in a lidded container, such as a pill bottle, and tape it shut before it goes in the trash.
- Use glue where fresh air moves. CA (super glue) fumes irritate the eyes, nose, and airways.
- Use small amounts of CA from a fine tip. It gives off heat as it sets, and a large amount on skin can burn.
- Work over a tray or card, not cloth. CA soaking into cotton or wool can heat up enough to burn you.

## Soldering and lead

- A soldering iron stays hot long after you unplug it. Always put it back in its stand.
- If the iron falls, let it fall. Never try to catch it.
- Pull rosin smoke away from your face with fume extraction, a small fan with a filter. Rosin fumes irritate the lungs and can cause asthma over time.
- Use rosin-core electronics solder. Acid-core solder is for plumbing and eats away at wiring.
- Clean flux with isopropyl alcohol only after the iron is off and cool. Keep the alcohol capped and away from the iron, because it catches fire easily.
- Shrink tubing with a heat gun or the side of the iron, never an open flame.
- Wash your hands after handling any solder, leaded or lead-free, before you eat or drink.
- Some old solder, cast metal parts, and older paints contain lead. Do not sand, file, drill, or heat an unknown old material until you know what it is.
- On a finished layout, solder to rail only with approval and an experienced member beside you. Heat melts plastic ties and scenery.

## Mains power

- Track power is low voltage, about 12 to 22 volts on DCC. It carries little shock risk, but keep your hands dry, take off rings and watches, and turn the power off before you work.
- Wall power (mains, 120 volts in the US) can injure or kill. Hobby work never needs you inside it. Never open or work inside anything plugged into the wall.
- Never open a power supply. It can hold a charge even when unplugged. If one misbehaves, replace it.
- Before you touch wiring, unplug every power supply, including separate power for lights or switches. Tell everyone in the room.
- A metal tool across both rails makes a short. Good systems shut off fast, but a short left in place can make a wire or rail very hot.
- Set a multimeter to voltage before you touch anything powered. On other settings it acts like a wire and can short the track.
- Power microcontroller projects through USB. Never connect a board to track power or wall power.
- A static grass applicator makes a high-voltage field. If you have a pacemaker or other implanted medical device, do not use one or stand near one in use. Switch it off and discharge it before you touch the screen or pin.

## Batteries

- Batteries and the cameras they power get warm. Leave airflow and stop if anything gets hot.
- Charge only with the maker's charger or a compatible one it recommends, on a surface that cannot burn, away from anything flammable, and while you are there. Never charge a damaged or swollen battery.
- Stop using a battery that swells, leaks, smells, or gets hot.
- Recycle old rechargeable batteries at a drop-off, not in the trash.

## Spraying and solvents

Brush-painting with water-based acrylics needs only a room with good fresh air. Spraying and solvent products (enamel, lacquer, thinners, strippers) need more:

- Spray outdoors where the label allows, or in a spray booth that vents outdoors and is rated for your paint. A booth that filters air back into the room does not remove solvent vapor. Many small booths handle water-based paint only.
- Wear the respirator the SDS calls for. For solvents and most spraying, that usually means a NIOSH-approved respirator with organic vapor cartridges plus P95 or P100 filters.
- An N95 dust mask does not stop solvent fumes or oily mist.
- Fit-test the respirator and check the seal each time. Facial hair breaks the seal.
- Store the respirator and cartridges in a sealed bag, and replace cartridges on the maker's schedule, not when you smell solvent.
- Choose gloves from the SDS. Nitrile handles brief splashes of many paints, but not every solvent. Replace soaked gloves right away.
- Keep paint, thinner, alcohol, rags, and spray cans away from flames, pilot lights, heaters, and sparks. Never heat, puncture, or burn a spray can.
- Strip paint in a small covered container with eye protection and gloves, and do not leave it unattended.
- If you get a headache, dizziness, or irritation, move to fresh air.
- Take used solvent and soaked rags to household hazardous waste. Never pour paint or solvent down a drain.

## Resin and foam

- Catch foam and plaster dust where you make it with a HEPA vacuum. Wear a NIOSH-approved N95 or better dust mask when you cut or sand foam or plaster.
- Hot-wire foam cutting makes fumes a dust mask does not filter. Hot-cut only foam whose maker permits it, never unknown, PVC, or polyurethane foam. Work outdoors, or with a fan blowing fumes outside.
- For two-part resin or epoxy, wear eye protection and the gloves the SDS specifies. Measure accurately and ventilate as the label says.
- Avoid skin contact with epoxy. Repeated contact can cause allergies.
- Pour resin in thin layers, never deeper than the label allows. Thick pours can overheat.

## First aid

Keep a basic first aid kit at the bench. Most bench accidents are small, so stay calm.

For any emergency, call 911. For a chemical in the eye or a swallowed product, call Poison Control (in the US, 1-800-222-1222).

**Fingers glued together.** Do not pull them apart, because it can tear the skin. Soak them in warm, soapy water, then gently peel or roll the skin apart. If that is not enough, use a little acetone or a CA debonder labeled safe for skin, on unbroken skin only. Never use solvent near the eyes or mouth.

**CA or a chemical in the eye.** Do not force the eye open. Rinse it with lukewarm water for 15 minutes, then call Poison Control and follow their advice.

**Small cuts.** Press with a clean cloth until the bleeding stops, rinse with clean water, and cover it.

**Small burns.** Cool the burn under cool running water for about 10 minutes, without ice, and cover it loosely with a clean dressing.

**Fumes.** Move to fresh air. Get medical help if symptoms do not pass.

Get medical help for deep cuts, bleeding that will not stop, anything in the eye, large or blistering burns, and any electrical burn.
