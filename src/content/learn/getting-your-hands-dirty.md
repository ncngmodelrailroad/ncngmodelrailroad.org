Most beginner mistakes can be fixed, repainted, or rebuilt. A few cannot. This guide sorts the work into what to try, when to ask first, and what to leave alone without approval.

## Try it, ask first, or leave it alone

Experienced members on most shared layouts expect questions, and most would rather answer one than repair a mistake. Layouts have history you cannot see, such as past repairs, hidden wiring, and parts that look loose but are meant to move.

**Try it.** In these jobs, a mistake usually costs time and materials, not the model.

- Scenery on a detached practice board: ground cover, rocks, trees, and grass can usually be layered, scraped off, and redone.
- Your own paint on scrap or your own projects. Water-based acrylics clean up with water while wet, and a coat you applied can usually be painted over once dry.
- Unglued [ballast](/learn/glossary#ballast) on a practice board, which brushes up and repositions easily.
- Simple kits. Test-fit parts before gluing, because some glues grab almost at once.
- Dry [weathering](/learn/glossary#weathering) powders on a practice surface. Start light, and build it up. On a finished model, ask first, because matte finishes can hold pigment.

Practice first on cheap material: scrap styrene, plastic spoons, a used freight car from a swap meet, or clean loose wire for soldering.

**Ask first.** These are not off limits, but a quick question protects the layout and its history.

- Installed scenery, especially near track, wiring, structures, or [turnouts](/learn/glossary#turnout-switch). Loose ballast grains can jam turnouts and moving parts.
- Locomotives, brass models, and fine detail parts. Locomotives carry motors and gears, and those equipped for [DCC](/learn/glossary#dcc-digital-command-control) also carry a decoder. Brass models are often hand-built in small runs and hard to replace, and their details bend easily.
- Track alignment. A small bump at a rail joint can derail train after train. Report a problem spot before you try to adjust it.
- Painting or weathering a model you did not build.

**Do not change without approval.** These mistakes can be permanent.

- Factory paint and lettering. Damage may be hard or impossible to match, and solvents or stripping can ruin it.
- Glued ballast and scenery. Once glue sets, ballast can lock a turnout or foul the rails, and removing it risks damaging the track.
- Layout wiring and power. A change in one spot can affect trains far away. Do not disconnect, cut, or add any wire.
- Decoder settings. Changing or resetting a decoder can erase a locomotive's address, speed, and sound settings if nobody recorded them. Replacing firmware or sound files can need the maker's hardware and original files.
- Fragile parts on older kits, which may have no replacement.

Also, never lean on the [benchwork](/learn/glossary#benchwork), sit on its edge, or set heavy tools on it.

## Re-railing a car

Derailments happen often. Stop the train first, or ask the operator to stop it. Ask before touching a locomotive, a brass model, or any equipment you do not know. For an ordinary car you are allowed to handle:

1. Lift it by supporting it from underneath, without squeezing the body or touching handrails, ladders, or brake wheels.
2. Set one [truck](/learn/glossary#truck), the swiveling frame that holds the wheels, on the rails so each wheel's inner lip sits inside the rail.
3. Line up the second truck the same way.
4. Roll the car gently back and forth an inch. It should move freely without climbing the rail.
5. If the same car keeps derailing at the same spot, report it. That usually points to a track or wheel problem, not to anything you did.

## Safety basics

- **Blades.** Cut away from your body and your other hand, use a fresh blade, cap the knife or set it in a holder when you stop, and put used blades in a rigid, closed container.
- **Eyes.** Wear safety glasses when cutting wire, snipping metal, drilling, using power tools, or soldering.
- **Hot tools.** A soldering iron stays hot long after you unplug it. Return it to its stand, and never catch a falling iron.
- **Fumes.** For glues, paints, and solvents, follow the product label and safety data sheet (SDS), use ventilation that carries fumes outdoors, and move to fresh air if you feel a headache, dizziness, or irritation.
- **Unknown old materials.** Leaded solder, some cast parts, and some older finishes contain lead. Do not sand, file, drill, or heat an unknown old material until you know what it is.
- **Food.** Keep food and drink away from the bench, wipe up dust instead of sweeping it, and wash your hands before eating or drinking.
- **Lifting and limits.** Keep loads close to your body, do not twist while lifting, and ask for help with anything heavy or awkward. If a ladder or a crawl under the benchwork does not suit you, say so.

For tool, paint, and soldering specifics, see the [workbench tools](/learn/first-workbench-tools), [painting and weathering](/learn/painting-weathering), and [electronics and DCC](/learn/electronics-dcc-basics) guides.

## Before your first session

You do not need to buy a tool kit before volunteering. The layout has shared tools for layout work, including an NCE Power Cab, a simple DCC controller, at the workbench. Members run trains with the Engine Driver app on Android tablets, which work as handheld throttles. The tablets connect to JMRI, software that runs the layout from a computer connected over USB. Running trains can be an easy way to start, so ask whether it is an option for you.

- [Email us](/contact) before attending to confirm the date and ask what to bring. The [volunteer page](/volunteer) has more.
- Wear clothes you do not mind getting paint or glue on, and closed-toe shoes. Tie back long hair.
- Bring reading glasses or a magnifier if you use them, safety glasses if you have a pair, and a phone or notebook for notes and before photos.
- Ask: What should I avoid touching? Which shared tools can I use? How do I stop a train? Is there a scrap piece I can practice on?
