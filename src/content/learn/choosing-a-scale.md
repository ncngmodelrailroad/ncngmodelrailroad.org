Model trains come in different sizes, called scales. Your scale decides how much room you need, what you can buy, and what it costs. This guide helps you pick one, then buy your first trains.

## The short version

- For a first train at home, HO has the most choice. N fits small spaces. On30 is a lower-cost way into larger O scale trains.
- Measure your space first. The tightest curve your trains can take decides what fits.
- Decide between DC and DCC (two ways to power and control trains) before you buy a controller.
- Used trains save money. Test them before you buy.
- Our layout is On3, a rare scale. You can enjoy it at the club without buying any.
- **Volunteering?** You do not need your own tools. See [Before your first session](/learn/getting-your-hands-dirty#before-your-first-session).

## Scale and gauge, in brief

**[Scale](/learn/glossary#scale)** is how much smaller the model is than the real train.

**[Gauge](/learn/glossary#gauge)** is the distance between the two rails.

One scale can have more than one gauge. **[On3](/learn/glossary#on3)**, for example, is O scale (1:48) modeling [narrow gauge](/learn/glossary#narrow-gauge) track with real rails 3 feet apart.

New to all of this? Read [New to Model Railroading?](/learn/new-to-model-railroading) first.

## Why HO, N, or On30 makes a good start

**HO is the easiest choice if you have the room.** It has the most locomotives (the engines that pull the train) with digital control and sound built in, the most starter sets, and the most used trains for sale. A small oval of track fits on a 4 by 8 foot table.

**N suits small spaces.** N trains are about half the size of HO. A loop fits on a desk. Small parts are harder to handle.

**On30 is a cheaper way into big O scale trains.** They run on HO track (16.5 mm between the rails). Plastic On30 models cost far less than On3 brass.

On30 starter sets often use 18-inch radius HO curves. Still, check each locomotive's limits. O scale trains are much bigger than HO, so they need more room on curves, through turnouts (track switches), and past scenery.

## The common scales at a glance

The **radius** measures a curve from its center to the track. Smaller means tighter. A simple loop needs a space a little more than twice the radius across.

These are common compact curves, not limits for every model. Longer trains need wider curves, and wider curves run better.

| Scale | Ratio | Starter loop radius |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Z | 1:220 | 6 to 8 in. |
| N | 1:160 | 11 to 12.5 in. |
| HO | 1:87.1 | 18 in. (22 in. or more runs better) |
| S | 1:64 | about 20 in. |
| O | 1:48 | 13.5 to 18 in. (three-rail, O27 to O36), about 36 in. and up (two-rail) |
| Large scale / G | 1:20.3 to 1:32 | about 2 ft or more |

- **HO** has the widest range of ready-to-run models, control gear, kits, and parts.
- **N** is second only to HO for models with digital control and sound.
- **O** comes in two styles. Three-rail O is the classic toy train on tight track circles. Two-rail O looks more realistic but needs wide curves.
- **Large scale, often called G,** runs on 45 mm track, suits garden railways, and costs more per piece.

**Specialized choices.** These have fewer models for sale, so expect more second-hand shopping.

- **Z** is the smallest common scale. It has European, North American, and Japanese trains. Adding decoders is fiddly.
- **S** sits between HO and O.
- **HOn3** is HO scale narrow gauge on 10.5 mm track. It has a mix of plastic ready-to-run models (many second-hand), brass, and kits.
- **On3** runs on 0.750-inch (19.05 mm) track. **Sn3** runs on 0.563-inch (14.3 mm) track. Both rely more on kits and brass.

## An honest word about On3

Our layout models the Nevada County Narrow Gauge in On3, with digital control (DCC). On3 is a rare scale with far fewer ready-to-run models than HO. Expect small batches of brass models, kits, and second-hand shopping.

Brass is priced by rarity and condition. Even a used On3 brass locomotive often costs more than a top-end HO model.

At the club, you can see On3 running without buying any On3 equipment.

## Other easy ways in

LEGO trains and toy starter sets give a family a working train on day one. Check the box for whether a motor is included, and follow its age rating and battery instructions.

## How to get trains

### Starter set or separate pieces?

A **starter set** boxes a locomotive, cars, a loop of track, and a power pack (the controller). It gets you running fast. Buying **separate pieces** takes more planning but lets you pick a better locomotive and a controller you will keep.

### DC, DCC-ready, or DCC with sound

- **DC** (direct current) is the simple, older way. The controller changes the track voltage to set the speed. Every locomotive on the same stretch of track moves together.
- **DCC-ready** locomotives run on DC today. Most have a socket for adding a decoder later. A decoder is a small circuit board inside the locomotive that obeys its own commands.
- **DCC with sound** locomotives come with a decoder and a speaker already inside. They cost more, but they work out of the box with a [DCC](/learn/glossary#dcc-digital-command-control) system. DCC lets several trains share a track, each at its own speed. [Electronics and DCC basics](/learn/electronics-dcc-basics) explains more.

### New or used

Used mass-produced trains often sell well below new prices, but they come with no warranty. Older models may need a new motor, or a decoder for DCC.

Try swap meets, train shows, estate sales, online auctions, and club sales.

Before you put an unknown used locomotive on any layout, do two things. First, find out whether it is DC or DCC. Second, check it for damaged wiring or a short (a wiring fault that lets power flow where it should not).

Very old or toy-grade gear, such as brass-rail track and many Tyco-era locomotives, is a poor first DCC purchase. Brass rail tarnishes and stops power reaching the train. Older motors can be hard to separate electrically from the frame, which a decoder needs. Skip these unless the seller shows they work, or you want a project.

## First purchase checklist

1. Measure your space. Pick a scale that fits a loop with room to spare.
2. Check the minimum radius the locomotive needs.
3. Decide between DC and DCC before you buy a controller.
4. Choose nickel silver track, not brass. It stays cleaner.
5. Set a budget, and save some for track and scenery.

## Buying used checklist

- **Control type:** confirm DC or DCC, then test it on a matching controller. It should run smoothly at low speed in both directions. Walk away from grinding, overheating, or a burning smell.
- **Wheels and couplers:** wheels roll freely and sit the right distance apart. Couplers (the hooks that join cars) match your other cars.
- **Parts and finish:** look for missing details, bent parts, and chipped paint, especially on brass.
- **Electronics:** if the seller says it has DCC or sound, ask to see it work.

## Brands to start with

These are long-established examples, not endorsements. Check reviews of the exact item before you buy.

- **Track:** Kato Unitrack and Bachmann E-Z Track snap together and have the roadbed (the raised base under the track) built in. Choose a nickel silver line. Atlas is the common choice for flex track (long pieces you bend to shape) and turnouts.
- **Trains:** Kato has a long record of smooth-running N and HO locomotives. Athearn, Bachmann, and Walthers make a wide range of HO. Bachmann makes most plastic On30.
