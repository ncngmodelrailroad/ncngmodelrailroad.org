You do not need a workshop to start building models. A kitchen table, a few small tools, and two or three glues will carry you through your first kits and repairs. This page covers what to buy first, which glue to use, and what to do if something goes wrong.

## The short version

- Start with a hobby knife, cutting mat, sprue cutters, tweezers, a steel rule, glue, good light, and safety glasses.
- Use plastic cement for styrene, CA for metal and mixed materials, and white glue for wood and scenery.
- Cut away from yourself, and replace dull blades.
- If you glue your fingers together, soak them in warm, soapy water. Do not pull them apart.
- **Volunteering?** You do not need your own tools. See [Before your first session](/learn/getting-your-hands-dirty#before-your-first-session).

## The bare minimum

If you buy nothing else, start with these.

| Tool | First use | Look for | Safety note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hobby knife | Trimming parts and cutting strips | A slim handle and #11 blades listed as compatible with it | Cut away from your body, with your other hand behind the blade |
| Cutting mat | Protecting the table under every cut | Self-healing, at least letter-paper size | Keep the knife capped or in a holder when you set it down |
| Flush cutters (sprue cutters) | Snipping plastic parts off the frame | One flat cutting face and fine jaws | Plastic only, unless the maker rates them for wire |
| Fine-point tweezers | Holding parts too small for fingers | Tips that meet evenly when you squeeze | Grip gently; squeezing hard can launch a small part |
| Steel rule | Measuring and guiding straight cuts | A cork or rubber back and clear marks | Keep fingers on top of the rule, away from the edge you cut along |
| Plastic cement and CA (super glue) | Joining parts | Thin liquid cement for styrene, and CA with a fine tip | Use them where there is good airflow |
| Desk lamp | Seeing edges, seams, and glue | A bright LED with neutral or daylight color, on an arm you can aim | Place it in front of you or opposite your working hand |
| Safety glasses | Cutting, snipping, and drilling | A Z87 marking, which shows they meet the US ANSI Z87.1 standard | Wear them every time; snipped bits fly further than you expect |

That kit is enough to start many simple plastic kits. Check each kit's instructions, since some also call for small screwdrivers, files, or a drill. Add wood glue for wood kits.

Good light matters more than most beginners expect. Light from the side or front shows seams, glue blobs, and the edge of a part. Magnification helps at any age, and reading glasses a step stronger than you normally use are a cheap way to start.

### Knife and cutter habits

- **Replace a dull blade.** A dull blade needs more force and is more likely to slip.
- **Make several light passes** instead of one hard cut.
- **Never catch a falling knife.** Step back and let it drop.
- **Put old blades in a lidded container,** such as a pill bottle, and tape it shut before it goes in the trash.
- **Cut sprue a little away from the part,** then trim close. Use electronics flush cutters rated for soft wire on brass or phosphor-bronze wire, and heavier cutters on hard steel (music) wire, which chips finer cutters.

## What it costs

Prices are rough US ranges from when this guide was last updated (2026) and will drift. Use them for budgeting, not shopping.

- **Bare-minimum kit:** about $75 to $160, or about $55 to $120 if you already own a good desk lamp
- **Hobby knife with a few blades:** about $7 to $10
- **100-pack of #11 blades,** a bulk buy you can make later: about $30 to $45
- **Precision sprue cutters:** about $12 to $20. Electronics flush cutters cost less.
- **Pin vise and a set of #61 to #80 drills:** about $10 for a budget set, up to about $45 for higher-grade tools
- **Head-worn magnifier with flip-down lenses:** about $55 to $100. A simpler headband magnifier runs about $15 to $40.

## Which glue for which job

No single glue does everything. Match the glue to the two materials you are joining, and test anything new on a scrap part first.

| Joining | Use | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Styrene to styrene | Plastic cement | It fuses the surfaces. Thin liquid types wick into the joint. |
| Metal, resin, or mixed materials | CA | Thin CA sets in seconds. Gap-filling CA gives you time to position parts. |
| Wood, paper, card, scenery | White or wood glue (PVA) | Dries nearly clear and stays a little flexible. |
| Heavy parts or joints that take a load | Epoxy | Mix two parts. It is stronger and less brittle than CA. |
| Clear windows and lenses | Canopy glue or clear parts cement | Look for a label that says it dries clear and is safe for clear plastic. |

Plastic cement will not bond metal, wood, resin, or slippery plastics such as acetal (for example, Delrin) or nylon. Pin or screw those instead.

Three extras make CA easier to control: fine applicator tips, accelerator (a spray that sets CA within seconds; use it sparingly), and debonder (a solvent that softens it).

Keep CA away from three things:

- **Clear parts.** CA fumes leave a white haze on windows and lenses.
- **Cotton or wool.** CA soaking into cotton cloth or cotton balls can heat up fast enough to burn you. Work over a tray or card, not a cloth.
- **Moving parts.** A drop wicking into a gear, truck, or motor can freeze it solid.

## Setting up your workspace

A good bench is less about space and more about habits. Five habits cover most of it:

1. **Cover the table** with your cutting mat or a sheet of hardboard.
2. **Work over a light-colored tray with a lip,** so small parts cannot roll away and are easy to spot.
3. **Sort small parts** in a pill organizer or tackle box, and leave kit parts on the sprue until you need them.
4. **Ventilate.** CA fumes can irritate the eyes, nose, and airways. Follow the label on any glue or solvent, and use them in a well-ventilated area. If you add a fan, aim it to carry vapors away from your face and other people, toward the outdoors, and away from any flame or heat.
5. **Keep glues, solvents, and blades capped** and out of reach of children and pets.

## If something goes wrong

### CA on skin or in the eye

- A large amount of CA on skin can cause a chemical burn, because it gives off heat as it sets. Use small amounts with a fine tip.
- **If you glue your fingers together, do not pull them apart.** Soak them in warm, soapy water, then gently peel or roll the skin apart. It can take a while.
- If soaking is not enough, use a little acetone or a CA debonder whose label says it is safe for skin, on unbroken skin only. Never use any solvent near the eyes or mouth, and keep it off your models, since it mars plastic and paint.
- **If CA gets in your eye,** do not force the eye open. Rinse it with lukewarm water for 15 minutes, then call Poison Control right away (in the US, 1-800-222-1222) and follow their advice about medical care.

### Cuts

- Keep a basic first aid kit at the bench.
- For a small cut, press on it with a clean cloth until it stops bleeding, rinse it with clean water, and cover it.
- Get medical help for deep cuts, cuts that will not stop bleeding, or anything in the eye.

## Nice to have later

These are the next four tools most people add:

- **Pin vise and #61 to #80 drills,** for grab irons and fine holes. Turn slowly, since small bits snap easily.
- **Needle files and sanding sticks,** for cleaning up edges and seams. Check often; you cannot put material back.
- **Jeweler's screwdrivers,** for the tiny screws in locomotives and cars. Match the tip to the screw and press down as you turn.
- **A headband magnifier,** for detail work when reading glasses are no longer enough.
