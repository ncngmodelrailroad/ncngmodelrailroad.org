Paint makes a shiny plastic model look like a real car that has worked for years. You need no spray gear to learn. Mistakes are normal: in your own paint, you can usually wipe, sand, or paint over them. Take extra care with factory paint and other people's models.

## The short version

- Start with a cheap practice car, water-based acrylic paint (it cleans up with water), and three brushes.
- Several thin coats look better than one thick coat.
- You can fix most mistakes in your own paint.
- Brush-painting with water-based paint needs only a room with fresh air. Spraying and stronger paints need more safety gear, covered near the end.
- **Volunteering?** You do not need your own tools. See [Before your first session](/learn/getting-your-hands-dirty#before-your-first-session).

## Your first project: paint a practice car

Get a cheap secondhand car or a spare kit (a model you build from parts). Work in a room with fresh air.

1. **Clean it.** Wash it in warm, soapy water and let it dry. This removes finger oils and **mold release**, a slippery factory coating that paint does not stick to.
2. **Prime it.** **Primer** is a first coat that helps paint grip. Brush it on, or spray it following the safety rules below. Pick one labeled for your model's material, in light gray for most colors. Use two light coats.
3. **Paint it.** Add water, or the paint maker's thinner, one drop at a time until the paint flows easily. Brush on two or three thin coats. Thick paint fills in small details like **rivets**, the tiny raised bumps that show where metal parts join.
4. **Letter it.** Add a decal or two over a glossy clear coat (see Decals below).
5. **Weather and seal it.** Add a wash or some drybrushing, then a flat clear coat to tie it together.

Keep this car to test each new technique.

## Brushes

- **A fine round brush** (size 0 or 10/0) for small details like handrails and lamps
- **A medium round brush** (size 1 or 2) for general painting
- **A small flat brush** for broad, even areas
- **An old, stiff brush** for drybrushing and powders, because those jobs wear brushes out

Synthetic (man-made) bristles suit acrylic paint. Dip only the bristles, not the metal band that holds them (the **ferrule**). Paint that dries there spreads the tip apart.

Rinse often while you work. When you finish, wash the brush with brush soap, shape the tip to a point, and let it dry lying flat so water or solvent cannot run into the ferrule.

## Decals

A **decal** is printed lettering or a logo for your model. Most are **water-slide decals**: a thin printed film on backing paper that slides off after a short soak in water. **Dry transfers**, rubbed on from a plastic sheet, are another option.

1. Brush a **gloss** (shiny) clear coat where the decal goes. A flat surface is rough up close and traps air under the film, a silvery haze called **silvering**.
2. Soak the decal briefly, until it slides on its backing.
3. Brush a mild **setting solution** onto the spot. This liquid helps the decal settle. Slide the decal into place with a soft brush.
4. Brush a stronger softening solution over it to help it hug rivets and seams, then leave it alone. Strong solutions can bend thin decals out of shape, so test on a spare first.
5. Let it dry overnight.

## Weathering and clear coats

**[Weathering](/learn/glossary#weathering)** makes a model look used, with dirt, rust, soot, and faded paint. Study photos of real equipment first, such as our [trains](/trains). Dirt collects low, and rust starts where water sits.

- **Washes.** Very runny dark paint that flows into seams to add shadow. Brush it on, then wipe the raised spots with a tissue.
- **Drybrushing.** Wipe almost all the paint off an old brush, then flick it across raised details so only the edges catch color.
- **Powders and pastels.** Brush on hobby weathering powder, or dust scraped from a dry soft pastel stick (not an oil pastel). Test first, because some powders stain flat paint. Do not blow powder off or stir up dust.

A **flat** (not shiny) clear coat goes on last. It hides decal edges and protects the paint. Use a hobby clear coat whose label says it works over your paint and decals. Test it on scrap first, because general-purpose clear coats can damage hobby paint. A flat coat can also fade powder effects, so apply it lightly. If you spray a clear coat, follow the same safety rules as any spraying.

## How to fix mistakes

These fixes are for paint you applied yourself. Ask the owner before you work on someone else's model.

- **Wet paint in the wrong spot:** lift water-based paint with a damp cotton swab. For other paints, use the matching cleaner, tested on the finish underneath.
- **A run, a drip, or a speck of dust:** let it dry, sand it smooth with fine sandpaper or a sanding stick, and repaint.
- **Too much weathering:** lift loose powder with a clean, dry brush. To soften a heavy wash, use a swab and a thinner that will not dissolve the paint beneath it.

**Stripping is a last resort.** A stripper is a liquid that removes all the paint. Strippers, even isopropyl (rubbing) alcohol, can damage plastic, clear window parts, resin, glued joints, and lettering. If you must strip a model:

1. Pick a stripper whose label allows your model's material, and test a hidden spot first.
2. Work in a small covered container, with eye protection, suitable gloves, and good fresh air.
3. Keep it away from flames and heaters, and do not leave it unattended.

## Paint types, for reference

Each type of paint uses a different **solvent**, the liquid that thins it and cleans it up. The label names the right one.

- **Water-based acrylic.** Cleans up with water, low smell, dries fast. The easiest start.
- **Alcohol-thinned acrylic.** Some hobby acrylics need the maker's alcohol-based thinner instead of water.
- **Enamel.** Cleans up with mineral spirits or enamel thinner. Strong fumes, slow drying, tough finish.
- **Lacquer.** Uses lacquer thinner. It dries fast and hard, but its solvents require strict ventilation and a respirator (a mask that filters the air you breathe).

Some railroad paints come in separate versions for plastic and bare metal. On brass, remove oil, tarnish, and soldering flux (a paste used when joining metal parts). Then use a primer labeled for bare metal, because primers made only for plastic may not grip.

**Test before you layer.** A strong solvent can lift or wrinkle an earlier coat, even one that feels dry. Follow each maker's advice on which products work together and drying times, and test the full combination on scrap. One brand of water-based acrylic avoids most of these problems.

## Spray cans and airbrushes

**Spray cans** work well for primer, one-color jobs, and clear coats. Spray short, light passes at the distance and temperature on the label. Never heat, puncture, or burn a can.

An **airbrush** is a small paint sprayer powered by an air compressor. It gives finer control but takes practice. Follow the maker's advice for thinner, mixing ratio, and air pressure, and practice on paper first. Many modelers never need one.

## What to buy

Prices are rough US ranges from when this guide was last updated (2026) and will drift. Use them for budgeting, not shopping.

**Start with brushes.** Brushes, primer, and four paints cost roughly $35 to $55. The other supplies add roughly $40 to $60.

- **Brushes:** synthetic bristles that hold a sharp point. A three-brush set costs about $12 to $20.
- **Primer:** light gray, labeled for your model's material, about $8 to $15.
- **Paint:** water-based hobby acrylics in black, a boxcar red or brown, a gray, and a rust color, about $3 to $5 a bottle.
- **Supplies:** the maker's thinner, gloss and flat hobby clear coats, decal setting solutions (about $5 a bottle), weathering powder, cotton swabs, and sanding sticks.

**Only if you decide to spray or use solvent paint:**

- **For spraying, a spray booth that vents outdoors,** about $120 to $250. A spray booth is a box with a fan that pulls fumes away from you. Its maker must approve it for the paint and solvent you use. Many small booths handle water-based paint only.
- **A NIOSH-approved half-face respirator,** about $40 to $60, with the cartridges and filters the product or conditions require. NIOSH is the US agency that tests and approves respirators.
- **Optional for spraying:** an airbrush and compressor set, about $90 to $180, with a pressure regulator and moisture trap.

## Brands to start with

These are long-established examples, not endorsements. Check reviews of the exact item before you buy.

- **Paint:** Vallejo, Reaper, and Citadel water-based acrylics.
- **Decal solutions:** Microscale Micro Set (mild) and Micro Sol (stronger).
- **Weathering powders:** Bragdon and AIM.
- **Airbrushes, if you get there:** Badger and Iwata.

## Safety

Follow the label and the **safety data sheet (SDS)** for every paint, thinner, clear coat, and stripper. The SDS is the maker's document listing hazards and the protection you need.

**Brush-painting with water-based acrylics** needs only a room with good fresh air. Keep food and drink off the paint bench, keep paint away from children and pets, and do not pour paint down a drain.

### If you spray or use solvent paints

- **Control spray at the source.** Spray outdoors where the label allows, or in a booth that vents outdoors and is rated for the product. A booth that only filters air and blows it back into the room does not remove solvent vapor.
- **Wear the respirator the product or conditions require.** For solvent paints and most spraying, the SDS usually calls for a NIOSH-approved respirator with **organic vapor** cartridges plus P95 or P100 filters. An N95 dust mask, or any other N-series filter, does not stop solvent fumes or oily mist.
- **Make the respirator work.** Pick the size with a fit test. Check the seal every time you put it on. Keep the area where it seals clean-shaven, because hair breaks the seal. Store the respirator and its cartridges in a sealed bag. Replace cartridges on the maker's schedule, not when you start to smell solvent.
- **Choose gloves from the SDS.** Disposable nitrile gloves handle brief splashes of many paints, but not every solvent. Replace soaked gloves right away.
- **Keep flammables away from fire and sparks.** Keep paint, thinner, alcohol, rags, and spray cans away from flames, pilot lights, heaters, and sparks.
- **Dispose of waste properly.** Do not pour paint or solvent down a drain. Take used solvent and soaked rags to a household hazardous waste program.
