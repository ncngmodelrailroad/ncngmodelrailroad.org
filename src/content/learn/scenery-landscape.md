By the end of this guide you can build a small hillside with dirt, grass, and bushes on a scrap board. Scenery is one of the most forgiving parts of the hobby. If a patch looks wrong, scrape it off and try again.

## Your first scenery patch

Start on a scrap board about a foot square. Build in layers: shape, paint, dirt, then bushes. Each layer hides mistakes in the one below.

1. **Shape a hill.** Crumple newspaper into a low mound and tape it down. Dip strips of plaster cloth (gauze coated with dry plaster) in water and lay them over the mound, overlapping each strip. Let it dry fully.
2. **Paint it.** Brush on flat (not shiny) earth-colored paint.
3. **Sprinkle while wet.** While the paint is still wet, sprinkle your finest dirt or foam over it. Let it dry, then tip the board and tap off the loose bits.
4. **Add texture.** Sprinkle a coarser layer in patches. Leave some bare dirt showing.
5. **Glue it down.** Mist it with wet water, then drip on scenery glue until it soaks through.
6. **Add bushes.** Press a few clumps of coarse foam into the wet glue near the bottom of the hill.
7. **Leave it overnight.** In the morning, compare it to a photo of a real hillside and redo anything that looks off.

Keep the board. It becomes your test patch for new colors and products.

Rules for this project: wear safety glasses when you sprinkle or spray, and keep scenery tools and containers out of the kitchen.

## The glue trick: bonded scenery

Modelers glue loose ground cover, called **bonded scenery**, with two liquids.

- **Wet water** soaks in instead of beading up. Add two or three drops of dish soap per cup of water.
- **Scenery glue** is white glue thinned with water plus a drop of soap. Start around one part glue to two parts water, test it on scrap, and adjust. Bottled scenery glue that dries clear and flat does the same job.

The method:

1. Sprinkle the material in place and shape it with a soft brush.
2. Mist it with wet water from above until it looks darker.
3. Drip the glue on with an eyedropper until it soaks through.
4. Let it dry overnight. Milky glue turns clear as it dries.

To redo a patch, dampen it with a little water, let it soften, and lift it with a putty knife.

## Shaping hills

For bigger hills, use stiff foam insulation board from a home center. Stack and glue pieces, then carve them with a long, thin knife, a rasp (a coarse file), and coarse sandpaper. Use white glue or a glue labeled safe for foam, because many spray glues and construction adhesives melt it.

Keep slopes gentle so ground cover stays put.

Rules for carving: cut away from your hand, and vacuum the crumbs as you go instead of sweeping them into the air. Sanding foam and plaster makes fine dust, so work with a window open. For dust masks, hot-wire foam cutters, and casting resin, see [Workshop Safety](/learn/workshop-safety#resin-and-foam).

## Ground cover

- **Scenic dirt.** Buy packaged scenic soil or clean craft sand instead of digging up your own. Soil from old mines can contain arsenic or mercury, and soil from industrial, roadside, or animal areas can carry other hazards.
- **Ground foam.** Dyed foam crumbs, from fine "turf" to coarse clumps for bushes. Mix two or three dull, earthy shades so it does not look like carpet.
- **[Ballast](/learn/glossary#ballast).** The crushed stone under the track. It glues down the same way as dirt. Keep glue and stone out of the moving parts of [turnouts](/learn/glossary#turnout-switch) and off the tops of the rails, because trains pick up power through clean metal.
- **Rocks.** Pour hard plaster (Hydrocal is the classic) into flexible rubber rock molds. Color the castings with thin, watery coats of earth-colored paint. Crush leftovers for talus, the broken stone at the foot of a cliff.

## Trees and bushes

- **Bushes:** clumps of coarse foam, glued where the ground meets rocks, fences, and buildings.
- **Leafy trees:** dust a real twig or a dried plant sold as sea foam with ground foam. Plant trees in uneven groups, not rows.
- **Pine trees:** twisted-wire "bottle-brush" trees, or a tree kit, make a good first try.

## Copy the real thing

The club models the Nevada County Narrow Gauge, which ran through the Sierra foothills. Grass on the open lower slopes turns green after autumn rains and dries to gold by summer. Oaks and gray pines dot the lower hills, with taller pines and firs higher up.

Work from photos. Notice how few colors there really are. When in doubt, go duller.

## Working on a real layout

A practice board can take any mistake. A finished layout needs more care:

- Ask first. On a shared layout such as the club's, get permission before you start.
- Move locomotives and cars out of the work area, and turn off all power.
- Cover rails and turnouts with painter's tape, and add liquids one drop at a time.
- Wait until everything is dry before the power goes back on.

## Try later

**Static grass.** Short nylon fibers that an electric applicator makes stand upright, like real grass. In O scale, 2 to 4 mm fibers look like grazed grass and 10 to 12 mm like tall dry grass. Follow the maker's instructions, and switch the applicator off before you touch its screen or pin. If you have a pacemaker or other implanted medical device, do not use one or stand near one in use.

**Water.** Most of the look comes from the paint under it. Pour the water product in thin layers, as its label directs. Resin products need extra care; see [Workshop Safety](/learn/workshop-safety#resin-and-foam).

## What to buy

If you plan to volunteer, buy nothing before your first club visit; the club has shared tools. For a practice board at home, a starter kit costs roughly $50 to $100:

- Plaster cloth and newspaper or foam scraps
- Flat earth-colored acrylic or latex paint and a cheap wide brush
- Two or three shades of fine ground foam, some coarse foam, and packaged scenic soil
- White glue, dish soap, a fine spray bottle, and an eyedropper
- Safety glasses and painter's tape

Optional upgrades: rubber rock molds, tree kits, a static grass applicator, and a water product. Woodland Scenics sells most of these in beginner sizes. Prices are rough and change over time.
