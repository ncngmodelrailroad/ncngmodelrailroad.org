This guide helps you measure your space, pick a scale that fits, and buy a first train that runs. Volunteering? Buy nothing before your first club visit; the club has shared tools at the layout.

## Step 1: Measure your space

Model trains come in sizes called **[scales](/learn/glossary#scale)**. Smaller trains take tighter curves, and the curve decides how much room you need. Measure three things:

1. **The width you have.** A loop needs a little more than twice the curve's radius across, plus a few inches of edge.
2. **How far you can reach.** Keep track within about 2 feet of the edge you stand at, 30 inches at most. Track beyond that is hard to clean or rerail.
3. **Where you will stand.** Leave a walkway of about 2 feet or more along every side you need to reach from.

That is why the classic 4 by 8 foot table is a poor first choice. You cannot reach its middle from one side, so it needs open floor on three or four sides and fills most of a spare bedroom.

## Step 2: Pick a footprint

These are realistic sizes for a simple loop on tight starter curves. Wider curves run better, so add room if you can.

| Scale | Size vs. real train | Tight starter curve (radius) | Loop footprint, about |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| N | 1:160 | 11 in. | 2.5 by 3 ft |
| HO | 1:87 | 18 in. | 3.5 by 5 ft |
| On30 | 1:48 | 18 in. | 3.5 by 5 ft, with big trains |
| O, three-rail | 1:48 | 13.5 to 18 in. | 3 to 4 ft across |

Add your walkway to every number. Even a 2 by 3 foot N board needs room around it to stand.

### Two good ways to start small

- **A shelf layout along a wall.** A board 12 to 18 inches deep fits in almost any room, and you can reach all of it. It holds no loop. Instead, you run a train back and forth and move cars in and out of short tracks, which keeps people busy for years.
- **A temporary loop on the floor or a table.** Snap-together track with the base built in, such as Kato Unitrack or Bachmann E-Z Track, sets up on the floor or a dining table and packs away in a box. You learn what you enjoy before you build anything permanent.

## Step 3: Choose a scale

- **HO** has the most starter sets, used trains, and locomotives with sound built in. Start here if you have the room.
- **N** is about half the size of HO and suits small rooms. The small parts take steadier hands.
- **On30** gives you large O scale trains on HO track. Plastic On30 models cost far less than brass, but the bigger trains need extra clearance past scenery and through turnouts (track switches).
- **Three-rail O** is the classic sturdy toy train on tight circles.

Z, S, and large garden scales have fewer models for sale.

### About the club's scale

Our layout models the Nevada County Narrow Gauge in **[On3](/learn/glossary#on3)**, with digital control. That is O scale on [narrow gauge](/learn/glossary#narrow-gauge) track, with real rails 3 feet apart. It is rare, with few ready-to-run models and costly brass. You can enjoy On3 at the club without buying any.

## Step 4: Buy your first train

### Starter set or separate pieces

A **starter set** puts a locomotive, cars, a loop of track, and a controller in one box, the fastest way to get running. **Separate pieces** take more planning but let you pick a better locomotive and a controller you will keep.

### How the train is controlled

- **DC** is the simple, older way. The controller changes the track power, and every train on that track moves together.
- **DCC-ready** locomotives run on DC today, and most have a socket for adding a decoder later. A decoder is a small circuit board that lets the locomotive obey its own commands.
- **DCC with sound** locomotives have the decoder and a speaker inside. They cost more but work with any [DCC](/learn/glossary#dcc-digital-command-control) system, which lets several trains share a track at their own speeds.

Decide between DC and DCC before you buy a controller. [Electronics and DCC Basics](/learn/electronics-dcc-basics) explains more.

### What to look for

Choose nickel silver track, since brass rail tarnishes and stops power reaching the train. Check that the locomotive can take your tightest curve.

## Buying used

Swap meets, train shows, estate sales, and online auctions sell trains well below new prices, with no warranty. Test before you pay.

- **Run it.** Confirm whether it is DC or DCC, then run it on a matching controller. It should move smoothly at low speed in both directions. Walk away from grinding, overheating, or a burning smell.
- **Check the details.** Wheels roll freely, couplers (the hooks that join cars) match your other cars, and no wires hang loose.
- **Skip old toy-grade gear** with brass-rail track as a first DCC purchase, unless you want a repair project.

Most problems are fixable, and fixing them is a good way to learn.

Prices are rough and change over time.
