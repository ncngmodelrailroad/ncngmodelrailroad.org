Scenery is everything around the track: hills, dirt, grass, rocks, and trees. It is one of the most forgiving parts of the hobby. On a practice board, you fix most mistakes by scraping off the patch and trying again. Work on a finished layout (the full model railroad) needs more care, covered under Working safely.

## The short version

- Build in layers: shape, paint, dirt and grass, rocks, trees, then small details.
- Practice first on a scrap board about a foot square. Mistakes there scrape off.
- Hold loose material in place with soapy water and watered-down white glue.
- Copy photos of real hillsides. Use duller colors than you think you need.
- On a finished layout, ask first and turn the power off.
- **Volunteering?** You do not need your own tools. See [Before your first session](/learn/getting-your-hands-dirty#before-your-first-session).

## Work in layers, from the ground up

Model scenery builds up in layers, like real ground. Each new layer hides mistakes in the one below.

1. **Landforms:** the shape of the land, such as hills and valleys.
2. **Base color:** earth-colored paint over that shape.
3. **Ground cover:** fine textures that look like dirt and grass.
4. **Rocks and talus:** talus is the pile of broken stone at the foot of a cliff.
5. **Bushes and trees:** the tall things.
6. **Details:** fences, stumps, fallen logs, and tiny people.

## Your first scenery patch

Start on a scrap board about a foot square. The sections below explain each material.

1. **Shape it.** Cover a small hill of foam or crumpled newspaper with plaster cloth. Let it dry fully.
2. **Paint it.** Brush on flat (not shiny), earth-colored paint.
3. **Sprinkle while wet.** While the paint is still wet, sprinkle your finest dirt or foam over it.
4. **Let it dry.** Then tip the board and tap off the loose material.
5. **Add texture.** Sprinkle a second, coarser layer in patches. Leave some bare dirt showing.
6. **Glue it down.** Mist it with wet water, then drip on scenery glue until it soaks through.
7. **Add bushes.** Press a few clumps of coarse foam into the wet glue near the bottom of the hill.
8. **Leave it overnight.**
9. **Look again in the morning.** Compare it to a photo of a real hillside, and redo anything that looks wrong.

Keep the board for testing new products and colors.

## Landforms: shaping the hills

You need a light, solid shape to build on:

- **Foam board.** Stiff foam insulation sheet from a home center. Stack and glue pieces to make hills. Use a glue labeled safe for foam, or white glue. Glues with strong solvents, including many construction adhesives and spray glues, melt foam. Carve the foam with a long, thin knife, a rasp (a coarse file), and coarse sandpaper.
- **Plaster cloth.** Gauze strips coated with dry plaster, sold for crafts and model scenery. Dip a strip in water and lay it over newspaper, cardboard strips, or foam. Let it dry fully.
- **Hardshell.** The older, cheaper method: dip paper towels in thin plaster and lay them over a web of cardboard strips.

Once the shell is hard, texture it with more plaster or a paper-and-plaster mix (Sculptamold is a common example). Keep slopes gentle so ground cover stays put.

## Earth colors and ground cover

**Paint first.** Brush flat, earth-colored latex or acrylic paint over the dry landforms. It hides white plaster if something chips, and wet paint holds the first layer of ground cover.

**Then add texture.** Ground cover comes in a few main types:

- **Scenic dirt and fine sand.** Buy packaged scenic soil or clean craft sand. Do not dig up unknown soil. In Gold Country, old mine waste can contain arsenic and mercury. Soil from mining, industrial, roadside, or animal-use areas can carry those or other harmful substances.
- **Ground foam.** Dyed foam crumbs, in sizes from fine "turf" to coarse clumps for bushes. Mix two or three dull, earthy shades so it does not look like carpet.
- **[Ballast](/learn/glossary#ballast).** The crushed stone under the track. Pick ballast sized for your scale (the size of your model trains), with uneven grains and some natural color variation. Test stone ballast with a magnet before you use it near track. Keep it out of the moving parts of [turnouts](/learn/glossary#turnout-switch), the track switches that send a train one way or the other.
- **Talus and rock castings.** Make rock faces by pouring a hard plaster (Hydrocal is the classic example) into flexible rubber molds. Color the rocks with thin, watery coats of earth-colored paint or an alcohol-based stain. Crush leftover castings to make instant talus.

## Bonded scenery: the glue trick

The standard way to glue loose ground cover, often called **bonded scenery**, uses two liquids:

- **Wet water.** Water that soaks in instead of beading up. Add two or three drops of dish soap per cup, or mix about one part 70% rubbing alcohol to four parts water.
- **Scenery glue.** White glue thinned with water, plus a drop of soap, or thinned acrylic matte medium. Recipes vary. Start around one part glue to two parts water, test it on scrap, and adjust. Bottled scenery glues labeled clear-drying and not glossy do the same job.

The method:

1. Sprinkle the loose material in place and shape it with a soft brush.
2. Mist it with wet water from a fine-mist spray bottle until it looks darker. Spray from above so you do not blow the material around.
3. Drip the scenery glue on with a pipette or eyedropper until it soaks through.
4. Let it dry overnight. It looks milky while wet and turns clear as it dries.

Ballast glues down the same way. Test on a scrap board first. Keep glue out of the moving parts of turnouts and off the tops of the rails, because trains need clean metal to pick up power.

To redo a small patch glued with white glue, first check what is underneath, such as wiring, track, or a turnout. Dampen only that patch, with as little water as it takes. Let it soften, then lift it with a putty knife.

## Trees and bushes

- **Bushes:** clumps of coarse foam or fiber. Glue them where the ground meets rocks, fences, and buildings.
- **Leafy trees:** start with an armature (a plastic or wire tree skeleton), a dried natural plant often called sea foam, or a real twig. Dust it with ground foam for leaves.
- **Pine-type trees (conifers):** use twisted-wire "bottle-brush" trees, or wrap a wooden dowel in fiber and sprinkle on fine foam. Tree kits make a good first try.

Plant trees in uneven groups, not rows.

## Look at the real thing

The Nevada County Narrow Gauge ran through the Sierra foothills. Grass on the open lower slopes usually turns green after the autumn rains and dries to gold by late spring or summer. Oaks and gray pines dot the lower hills. Higher up, ponderosa pine, Douglas-fir, black oak, and incense-cedar take over.

Work from photos. Notice how grass grows thicker along fences, and how few colors there really are. When in doubt, go duller.

## Try later

**Static grass.** The usual next step. Static grass is short nylon or polyester fibers, about 1 mm to 12 mm long. In O scale (models at 1/48 of real size), 2 to 4 mm looks like grazed grass and 10 to 12 mm like tall dry grass. An electric applicator makes the fibers stand up. It creates a high-voltage electric field between its screen and a grounding pin you push into the wet glue. Makers warn about pacemakers. If you have a pacemaker or other implanted medical device, do not use one or stand near one in use. Follow the maker's safety instructions, and switch it off and discharge it before you touch the screen or pin.

**Water.** Most of the look of model water comes from the paint under it. Close any gaps with plaster cloth or a paintable acrylic caulk, not silicone. Paint the bed with 100% acrylic paint, because the vinyl in many latex and craft paints can react with water products. Seal it as the water product's maker directs. Then pour thin layers, never deeper than the label allows. Thick resin pours can overheat.

**Backdrop.** A plain sky-blue wall behind the layout makes it look bigger. Lighten the sky near the horizon, and keep distant hills pale and bluish.

## Working safely

On any project:

- Wear eye protection when cutting, carving, sanding, or spraying.
- Control dust. Ventilate. Catch dust where you make it with a HEPA-filtered vacuum or one rated for fine dust. Wear a NIOSH Approved N95 or better particulate respirator (a tight-fitting dust mask) when cutting or sanding foam and plaster.
- Keep scenery tools and containers out of the kitchen.

Only if you cut foam with a hot wire or pour resin:

- Hot-wire foam cutting makes fumes that a dust respirator does not filter. Hot-cut only foam whose maker permits it. Never hot-cut unknown, PVC, or polyurethane foam. Work outdoors, or with a fan that blows the fumes outside.
- For two-part resin, wear eye protection and chemical-resistant gloves of the type the resin maker or its safety data sheet (SDS) specifies. Measure accurately, and ventilate as the label requires. Repeated skin contact with epoxy can cause allergies.

On a finished layout, also:

- Ask first. On a shared layout such as a club's, get permission before you start.
- Move locomotives and cars out of the work area.
- Disconnect every power supply. That means the DCC system (the digital system that powers and controls the trains) and any separate power for lights or switches, not only the track power.
- Protect the track. Cover rails and turnouts with painter's tape. Cover nearby buildings and electronics. Keep a static-grass applicator's pin and screen away from rails and wiring.
- Control liquids and dust. Apply liquids one drop at a time, and vacuum as you go.
- Wait until everything is fully dry before you turn the power back on.

## Supplies for a first patch at home

Prices are rough US ranges from when this guide was last updated (2026) and will drift. Use them for budgeting, not shopping.

The bare minimum comes to about $60 to $110:

- Plaster cloth (about $10 a roll) and foam scraps or newspaper
- Flat earth-colored latex or acrylic paint and a cheap wide brush
- Two or three shades of fine ground foam (about $10 to $15 each) and some coarse foam
- Packaged scenic soil or clean craft sand
- White glue or acrylic matte medium, and dish soap or 70% rubbing alcohol
- A fine-mist spray bottle and a pipette or eyedropper
- Safety glasses and painter's tape

Optional upgrades:

- Static grass applicator: about $70 to $110 entry-level, up to about $190 premium. Fiber bags run about $5 to $10.
- Rubber rock molds and casting plaster: about $10 to $15 each
- Tree kits: about $15 to $25
- Water product: about $20 to $30 for 16 ounces, or about $10 to $15 for a small two-part resin kit

## Brands to start with

These are long-established examples, not endorsements. Check reviews of the exact item before you buy.

- **Plaster cloth, ground foam, rock molds, scenery glue, and water products:** Woodland Scenics.
- **Ground cover and trees:** Scenic Express.
- **Landforms:** extruded insulation foam board from a home center, and Sculptamold.
- **Rock castings:** Hydrocal.
