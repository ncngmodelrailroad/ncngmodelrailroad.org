Scenery turns a board with track into a place, and it is one of the more forgiving parts of the hobby. On a detached test board, most mistakes are cheap to fix: scrape off the patch and try again. Work on an installed layout needs more care, covered under Working safely.

## Work in layers, from the ground up

Model scenery builds up in layers, like real ground. Each layer hides mistakes in the one below.

1. **Landforms:** the hills, cuts, and valleys.
2. **Base color:** earth-toned paint over the shape.
3. **Ground cover:** fine textures like dirt and grass.
4. **Rocks and talus:** the broken stone that piles up at the foot of a cliff.
5. **Bushes and trees:** the tall stuff.
6. **Details:** fences, stumps, fallen logs, and people.

## Your first scenery patch

Practice on a scrap board about a foot square. The sections below explain each material.

1. **Shape it.** Cover a small hill of foam or crumpled newspaper with plaster cloth. Let it dry fully.
2. **Paint it.** Brush on flat, earth-colored paint.
3. **Sprinkle while wet.** While the paint is still wet, sprinkle your finest dirt or foam over it.
4. **Let it dry.** Then tip the board and tap off the loose material.
5. **Add texture.** Sprinkle a second, coarser layer in patches. Leave some bare dirt showing.
6. **Bond it.** Mist with wet water, then drip scenery glue until it soaks through.
7. **Add bushes.** Press a few clumps of coarse foam into the wet glue near the base of the hill.
8. **Leave it overnight.**
9. **Look again in the morning.** Compare it to a photo of a real hillside, and touch up or redo what looks wrong.

Keep the board as a test piece for new products and colors.

## Landforms: shaping the hills

You need a light, solid shape to build on:

- **Foam board.** Rigid extruded insulation foam from a home center stacks into hills. Glue the layers with an adhesive labeled safe for foam, or with white glue. Solvent-based glues, including many construction adhesives and spray glues, melt foam. Carve it with a long, thin knife, a rasp, and coarse sandpaper.
- **Plaster cloth.** Gauze strips coated with plaster, sold for crafts and for model scenery. Dip a strip in water and lay it over newspaper, cardboard strips, or foam. Let it dry fully.
- **Hardshell.** The older, cheaper version: paper towels dipped in thin plaster over a web of cardboard strips.

Once the shell is hard, smooth or roughen it with a paper-fiber and plaster compound (Sculptamold is a common example) or more plaster. Keep slopes gentle so ground cover stays put.

## Earth colors and ground cover

**Paint first.** Brush flat, earth-colored latex or acrylic paint over dry landforms. It hides white plaster if something chips, and wet paint grabs the first layer of ground cover.

**Then texture.** Ground cover falls into a few families:

- **Scenic dirt and fine sand.** Use packaged scenic soil or clean craft sand. Don't collect unknown soil. In Gold Country, old mine waste can contain arsenic and mercury, and soil from mining, industrial, roadside, or animal-use areas can carry those or other contaminants.
- **Ground foam.** Dyed foam crumbs, sold by texture from fine "turf" to coarse clumps for bushes. Blend two or three dull, earthy shades so it doesn't look like carpet.
- **[Ballast](/learn/glossary#ballast).** The crushed stone under the track. Look for ballast graded for your scale, with irregular grains and natural color variation. Test mineral ballast with a magnet before using it near track, and keep it out of the moving parts of [turnouts](/learn/glossary#turnout-switch).
- **Talus and rock castings.** Cast rock faces in flexible rubber molds with a hard casting plaster (Hydrocal is the classic example). Color them with thin washes of earth-tone paint or an alcohol-based stain. Crushed leftover castings make instant talus.

## Bonded scenery: the glue trick

The standard way to glue loose ground cover, often called **bonded scenery**, uses two liquids:

- **Wet water.** Water with two or three drops of dish soap per cup, or about one part 70% rubbing alcohol to four parts water. It breaks surface tension so glue soaks in instead of beading up.
- **Scenery glue.** White glue thinned with water, plus a drop of soap, or thinned acrylic matte medium. Recipes vary. Start around one part glue to two parts water, test it on scrap, and adjust. Bottled scenery glues labeled clear-drying and not glossy do the same job.

The method:

1. Sprinkle the loose material where you want it. Shape it with a soft brush.
2. Mist it with wet water from a fine-mist spray bottle until it darkens. Mist from above so you don't blow it around.
3. Drip the scenery glue on with a pipette or eyedropper until it soaks through.
4. Let it dry overnight. It looks milky while wet and clears as it dries.

Ballast bonds the same way. Test on a scrap board first, and keep glue out of turnout points and away from rail tops.

To redo a small patch bonded with white glue, first check what lies underneath, such as wiring, track, or a turnout. Dampen just that patch with as little water as it takes, let it soften, and lift it with a putty knife.

## Trees and bushes

- **Bushes:** clumps of coarse foam or fiber. Glue them where the ground meets rocks, fences, and structures.
- **Deciduous trees:** a plastic or wire armature, a dried natural plant (often called sea foam), or a twig, dusted with ground foam.
- **Conifers:** twisted-wire bottle-brush trees, or a wooden dowel wrapped in fiber and flocked. Tree kits make a good first try.

Plant trees in uneven groups, not rows.

## Look at the real thing

The Nevada County Narrow Gauge ran through the Sierra foothills. Grass on open lower slopes usually greens after the autumn rains and dries to gold by late spring or summer. Oaks and gray pines dot the lower hills, and ponderosa pine, Douglas-fir, black oak, and incense-cedar take over as the land climbs.

Work from photos. Notice how grass grows thicker along fences and how few colors there really are. When in doubt, go duller.

## Try later

**Static grass.** This is the usual next step after a first patch. Short nylon or polyester fibers come in lengths from about 1 mm to 12 mm. In O scale, 2 to 4 mm reads as grazed grass and 10 to 12 mm as tall dry grass. An electric applicator creates a high-voltage field between its screen and a grounding pin in the wet glue, so the fibers stand on end. Makers warn about pacemakers: if you have a pacemaker or other implanted medical device, don't use one or stand near one in use. Follow the maker's safety instructions, and switch it off and discharge it before touching the screen or pin.

**Water.** Most of the look of model water comes from the paint under it. Close gaps with plaster cloth or a paintable acrylic caulk, not silicone. Paint the bed with 100% acrylic paint, because the vinyl in many latex and craft paints can react with water products. Seal it as the water product's maker directs, then pour thin layers, never deeper than the label allows. Thick resin pours can overheat.

**Backdrop.** A plain sky-blue wall makes a layout look bigger. Lighten the sky near the horizon, and keep distant hills pale and bluish.

## Working safely

On any project:

- Wear eye protection when cutting, carving, sanding, or spraying.
- Control dust. Ventilate, collect dust at the source with a HEPA-filtered vacuum or one rated for fine dust, and wear a NIOSH Approved N95 or better particulate respirator when cutting or sanding foam and plaster.
- Hot-wire foam cutting makes fumes that a dust respirator does not filter. Hot-cut only foam whose maker permits it, never unknown, PVC, or polyurethane foam, and work outdoors or with exhaust that vents outside.
- For two-part resin, wear eye protection and chemical-resistant gloves specified by the resin maker or its safety data sheet (SDS). Measure accurately and ventilate as the label requires. Repeated skin contact with epoxy can cause allergies.
- Keep scenery tools and containers out of the kitchen.

On an installed layout, also:

- Ask first. On a shared layout such as a club's, get permission before you start.
- Remove locomotives and cars from the work area.
- Disconnect every supply: the DCC system and any separate accessory power, not just track power.
- Protect the track. Mask rails and turnouts with painter's tape, and cover nearby structures and electronics. Keep a static-grass applicator's pin and screen away from rails and wiring.
- Control liquids and dust. Apply liquids a drop at a time, and vacuum as you go.
- Wait until everything is fully dry before restoring power.

## Supplies for a first patch at home

Prices are rough US ranges from when this guide was last updated (2026) and will drift. Use them for budgeting, not shopping.

The bare minimum comes to about $50 to $70:

- Plaster cloth (about $10 a roll) and foam scraps or newspaper
- Flat earth-tone latex or acrylic paint and a cheap wide brush
- Two or three shades of fine ground foam (about $10 to $15 each) and some coarse foam
- Packaged scenic soil or clean craft sand
- White glue or acrylic matte medium, and dish soap or 70% rubbing alcohol
- A fine-mist spray bottle and a pipette or eyedropper
- Safety glasses and painter's tape

Optional upgrades:

- Static grass applicator: about $70 to $110 entry-level, up to about $190 premium. Fiber bags run about $5 to $10.
- Rubber rock molds and casting plaster: about $10 to $15 each
- Tree kits: about $15 to $25
- Water product: about $20 to $30 for 16 ounces, or about $10 to $15 for a small two-part resin kit
