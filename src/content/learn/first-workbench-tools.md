You do not need a workshop to build models. A kitchen table, a few small tools, and two or three glues are enough for your first kits and repairs. This page shows what to buy first and which glue to use. Mistakes are normal, and most are easy to fix.

## The short version

- Start with a hobby knife, a cutting mat, sprue cutters, tweezers, a steel ruler, glue, a good lamp, and safety glasses.
- Use plastic cement for plastic parts, super glue (CA) for metal and mixed materials, and white glue for wood and scenery.
- Cut away from yourself. Replace a blade when it gets dull.
- If you glue your fingers together, do not pull them apart. Soak them in warm, soapy water.
- **Volunteering?** You do not need your own tools. The club keeps shared tools at the layout. See [Before your first session](/learn/getting-your-hands-dirty#before-your-first-session).

## The bare minimum

Start with these. Three terms first:

- **Styrene** is the hard plastic most model kits are made of.
- **Sprue** is the plastic frame that kit parts come attached to, like a frame of puzzle pieces.
- **CA** (cyanoacrylate) is the chemical name for super glue.

| Tool | What it does | What to look for | Safety note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Hobby knife | Trims parts and cuts thin strips | A slim handle and #11 blades listed as compatible with it | Cut away from your body. Keep your other hand behind the blade. |
| Cutting mat | Protects the table under every cut | Self-healing (cuts close up), at least letter-paper size | When you set the knife down, cap it or put it in a holder. |
| Flush cutters (sprue cutters) | Snip parts off the sprue | One flat cutting face and fine jaws | Use them on plastic only, unless the maker says they can cut wire. |
| Fine-point tweezers | Hold parts too small for fingers | Tips that meet evenly when you squeeze | Grip gently. A hard squeeze can launch a small part. |
| Steel ruler | Measures and guides straight cuts | A cork or rubber back and clear marks | Keep your fingers on top of the ruler, away from the edge you cut along. |
| Plastic cement and CA (super glue) | Join parts | Thin liquid cement for styrene, and CA with a fine tip | Use them where fresh air moves through the room. |
| Desk lamp | Lets you see edges, seams, and glue | A bright LED with neutral or daylight color, on an arm you can aim | Place it in front of you, or on the side away from your working hand. |
| Safety glasses | Protect your eyes when you cut, snip, or drill | A Z87 marking, which shows they meet the US ANSI Z87.1 standard | Wear them every time. Snipped bits fly farther than you expect. |

That is enough for many simple plastic kits. Some kits also call for small screwdrivers, files, or a drill, so read the instructions first. For wood kits, add wood glue.

Good light matters more than beginners expect. Light from the side or front shows seams, glue blobs, and edges. Magnification helps at any age. Reading glasses one step stronger than usual are a cheap start.

### Knife and cutter habits

- **Replace a dull blade.** A dull blade needs more force, so it is more likely to slip.
- **Make several light passes** instead of one hard cut. It gives you more control.
- **Never catch a falling knife.** Step back and let it drop. A blade is cheaper than stitches.
- **Put old blades in a container with a lid,** such as a pill bottle. Tape it shut before it goes in the trash.
- **Cut sprue a little away from the part,** then trim close. This keeps you from nicking the part.
- **Match the cutter to the wire.** For soft wire such as brass or phosphor-bronze, use electronics flush cutters rated for soft wire. For hard steel wire (called music wire), use heavier cutters, because it chips finer cutters.

## What it costs

Prices are rough US ranges from when this guide was last updated (2026) and will drift. Use them for budgeting, not shopping.

- **Bare-minimum kit:** about $75 to $160, or about $55 to $120 if you already own a good desk lamp
- **Hobby knife with a few blades:** about $7 to $10
- **100-pack of #11 blades,** a bulk buy you can make later: about $30 to $45
- **Precision sprue cutters:** about $12 to $20. Electronics flush cutters cost less.
- **Pin vise and a set of #61 to #80 drills:** about $10 for a budget set, up to about $45 for higher-grade tools
- **Head-worn magnifier with flip-down lenses:** about $55 to $100. A simpler headband magnifier runs about $15 to $40.

## Which glue for which job

No single glue does everything. Find the two materials you are joining in this table. Test anything new on a scrap part first.

| Joining | Use | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Styrene to styrene | Plastic cement | It softens both surfaces so they fuse. Thin liquid types flow into the joint. |
| Metal, resin, or mixed materials | CA (super glue) | Thin CA sets in seconds. Thicker "gap-filling" CA gives you time to line parts up. |
| Wood, paper, card, scenery | White or wood glue (also called PVA) | It dries nearly clear and stays a little flexible. |
| Heavy parts, or joints that hold weight | Epoxy | You mix two parts together. It is stronger and less brittle than CA. |
| Clear windows and lenses | Canopy glue or clear parts cement | Look for a label that says it dries clear and is safe for clear plastic. |

Plastic cement will not bond metal, wood, resin, or slippery plastics such as acetal (for example, Delrin) or nylon. Pin or screw those instead.

Three extras make CA easier to control:

- **Fine applicator tips** put the glue where you want it.
- **Accelerator** is a spray that makes CA set within seconds. Use a little at a time.
- **Debonder** is a liquid that softens CA so you can undo a joint.

Keep CA away from three things:

- **Clear parts.** CA fumes leave a white haze on windows and lenses.
- **Cotton or wool.** CA soaking into cotton cloth or cotton balls can heat up fast enough to burn you. Work over a tray or a piece of card, not a cloth.
- **Moving parts.** A drop that flows into a gear, wheel set, or motor can freeze it solid.

## Setting up your workspace

A good bench is about habits more than space:

1. **Cover the table** with your cutting mat or a sheet of hardboard.
2. **Work over a light-colored tray with a raised edge.** Small parts cannot roll off and are easy to spot.
3. **Sort small parts** in a pill organizer or tackle box. Leave kit parts on the sprue until you need them.
4. **Get fresh air moving.** CA fumes can irritate the eyes, nose, and airways. Follow the label on any glue or solvent, and use them in a well-ventilated area. If you add a fan, aim it so it carries fumes away from your face and other people, toward the outdoors, and away from any flame or heat.
5. **Cap glues, solvents, and blades** and keep them out of reach of children and pets.

## If something goes wrong

Most bench accidents are small. Stay calm and follow these steps.

### CA on skin or in the eye

- CA gives off heat as it sets. A large amount on skin can cause a chemical burn. Use small amounts with a fine tip.
- **If you glue your fingers together, do not pull them apart.** Pulling can tear the skin. Instead:
  1. Soak the fingers in warm, soapy water.
  2. Gently peel or roll the skin apart. It can take a while.
  3. If soaking is not enough, use a little acetone or a CA debonder whose label says it is safe for skin, on unbroken skin only. Never use any solvent near the eyes or mouth. Keep it off your models too, since it damages plastic and paint.
- **If CA gets in your eye,** do not force the eye open.
  1. Rinse the eye with lukewarm water for 15 minutes.
  2. Call Poison Control right away (in the US, 1-800-222-1222).
  3. Follow their advice about medical care.

### Cuts

- Keep a basic first aid kit at the bench.
- For a small cut:
  1. Press on it with a clean cloth until it stops bleeding.
  2. Rinse it with clean water.
  3. Cover it.
- Get medical help for deep cuts, cuts that will not stop bleeding, or anything in the eye.

## Nice to have later

You do not need these on day one. They are the next four tools most people add:

- **Pin vise and #61 to #80 drills.** A pin vise is a small hand drill you turn with your fingers. Use it for tiny holes, such as the holes for grab irons (the small handrails on the sides of train cars). Turn slowly, because small drill bits snap easily.
- **Needle files and sanding sticks,** for smoothing edges and seams. Check often, because you cannot put material back.
- **Jeweler's screwdrivers,** for the tiny screws in locomotives and cars. Match the tip to the screw, and press down as you turn so the tip does not slip.
- **A headband magnifier,** for detail work when reading glasses are no longer enough.
