This guide gets you ready for your first visit to the layout and shows you what you can try once you are there. Most mistakes in this hobby are fixable. Paint comes off, scenery gets redone, and cars go back on the track.

## Before your first session

Start with an email. [Contact us](/contact) before you come so we can confirm a date and tell you what to expect. The [volunteer page](/volunteer) has more about how to help.

You do not need your own tools. Buy nothing before your first visit; the club has shared tools at the layout, including an NCE Power Cab, a simple DCC controller, at the workbench. Ask a member what work and which shared tools are available.

To get ready:

1. Wear clothes you do not mind getting paint or glue on, and closed-toe shoes. Tie back long hair.
2. Bring reading glasses or a magnifier if you use them, and safety glasses if you have a pair.
3. Bring a phone or notebook for notes and "before" photos.

When you arrive, a few good questions to ask: What should I avoid touching? Which shared tools can I use? How do I stop a train? Is there a scrap piece I can practice on?

## Running trains

Running trains can be an easy way to start, so ask whether it is an option for you. Members drive trains with the Engine Driver app on Android tablets. The tablets connect to JMRI, software on a computer that runs the layout over USB. If running trains is offered, ask how to start, slow down, and stop.

## What is fine to try

Practice on scrap and on your own projects. A mistake there costs a little time, and you can start again.

- **Scenery on a practice board.** Use a loose piece of wood or foam that is not part of the layout. Layer dirt, rocks, grass, and trees, scrape them off, and try again.
- **Acrylic paint on scrap or your own models.** Water-based acrylic paint cleans up with water while wet, and you can paint over it once dry.
- **Loose [ballast](/learn/glossary#ballast)** (the fine gravel under model track) on a practice board. Until you glue it, it brushes up easily.
- **Simple kits.** Fit the parts together without glue first, because some glues grab almost at once.
- **[Weathering](/learn/glossary#weathering) powders** on scrap. Weathering makes a model look used and dirty. Start light and add more.

## Ask first

A layout has history you cannot see, such as old repairs and hidden wires. A quick question protects it, and members would rather answer one than fix a surprise.

- Scenery already on the layout, especially near track or [turnouts](/learn/glossary#turnout-switch) (track switches). Loose ballast grains can jam moving parts.
- Locomotives, brass models, and anyone else's model. Brass models are often handmade and hard to replace.
- Track that looks out of line. Report the spot instead of adjusting it.

## Leave alone unless a member says otherwise

These are hard to undo, so they wait until someone shows you how.

- Layout wiring and power. Do not unplug, cut, or add any wire.
- Decoder settings. A decoder is the small circuit board inside a [DCC](/learn/glossary#dcc-digital-command-control) locomotive, and a reset can erase its number and sound settings.
- Glued ballast, factory paint, and lettering.

Also, do not lean on, sit on, or set heavy tools on the [benchwork](/learn/glossary#benchwork), the tables and frames that hold up the layout.

## Putting a car back on the track

Cars come off the track (derail) often. It is normal and easy to fix. Stop the train first, or ask the operator to stop it. Ask before you touch a locomotive, a brass model, or anything you do not recognize. For an ordinary car:

1. Lift the car by holding it from underneath. Do not squeeze the body or grab the handrails, ladders, or brake wheels.
2. Set one [truck](/learn/glossary#truck) on the rails. A truck is the swiveling frame that holds a set of wheels. Each wheel has a raised inner lip (the flange) that sits inside the rail.
3. Line up the second truck the same way.
4. Roll the car gently back and forth about an inch. It should roll freely and stay on the rails.
5. If the same car keeps coming off at the same spot, report it. That usually means a track or wheel problem, not something you did.

## A few safety habits

- Cut away from your body and your other hand, and cap the knife when you stop.
- Wear safety glasses when you cut wire, drill, or solder.
- Put a soldering iron back in its stand. If it falls, let it fall.
- Keep food and drinks off the bench, and wash your hands before you eat.
- Ask for help with anything heavy, and say so if crawling under the benchwork does not suit you.

For soldering, fumes, old materials, and first aid, see [Workshop Safety](/learn/workshop-safety).
